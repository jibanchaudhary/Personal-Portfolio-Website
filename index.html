Deep learning · Medical imaging

TI-RACS — thyroid nodule risk stratification

Radiologists grade thyroid nodules on the ACR TI-RADS scale, TR1 (benign) through TR5 (highly suspicious). It's a judgement call made from an ultrasound image, and it decides whether someone gets a biopsy.

TI-RACS is an image-based deep learning system for multi-class TI-RADS classification. The project benchmarks modern vision backbones, including ConvNeXt, EfficientNet, Swin Transformer and ViT, with Swin Transformer achieving the strongest performance in our experiments. The current research direction focuses on a Swin-based model with a learnable despeckling stem and echogenicity-aware attention to better capture clinically relevant ultrasound features. The pipeline includes XML based dataset construction, stratified cross-validation, ablation studies, and Grad-CAM visualization for interpreting model predictions.

What it doesn't do. TI-RACS is a research prototype, not a medical device, and must not be used to make clinical decisions. Strong validation metrics on a single dataset do not guarantee clinical reliability or generalization. The next step is evaluation against radiologist-annotated data collected across multiple institutions and ultrasound systems, with particular attention to robustness and external generalization.

PDFlow — Nepali PDF to editable Word

Ordinary PDF-to-Word converters destroy Nepali documents. Text set in legacy or CID fonts — subset Mangal, Preeti — extracts as garbage, and anything scanned arrives as a flat picture. For a country that runs on government forms, that's not a small annoyance.

PDFlow routes each file to one of two engines. Clean digital PDFs take the fast pdf2docx reflow path. Anything with undecodable Devanagari or scanned content goes to a layout engine that re-renders the region, reads it back with PaddleOCR, and rebuilds the page as coordinate-pinned, fully editable text boxes. It handles the awkward cases too: paragraphs trapped inside images, and banners where baked-in text has to be painted out so the logo survives while the heading becomes editable again.

Why it matters to me. This is the clearest example of the thing I keep coming back to: tools work fine in English and fail quietly in Nepali, and somebody has to do the unglamorous work of closing that gap.

Undergraduate research · ACEM · Dr. Nanda Bikram Adhikari

A legal advisor for cybercrime

Nepal's Electronic Transactions Act, 2063 governs most of what happens to people online here. Almost nobody I know has read it, and a first consultation with a lawyer costs more than many families will spend on a question they aren't sure is even legal.

A LangChain agent decides whether a question is legal at all. A fine-tuned Legal-BERT model classifies it, and a RAG layer built on sentence-transformer embeddings pulls the matching statutes out of a curated corpus. The answer comes back summarised, in words a non-lawyer can use.

What it doesn't do. The corpus is small and English-heavy — the wrong language for most people who'd need it. It gives legal information, not legal advice. Next time I'd start with Nepali coverage.

Research project · Deep learning, video

Deepfake detection that reads time, not stills

A single frame of a manipulated video can look perfect. The tell is usually across frames — a flicker at the jaw, a blink that lands wrong. So a pretrained XceptionNet backbone pulls spatial features out of every frame, and an LSTM reads those features as a sequence.

I wrapped it in a small Flask app: upload a clip, get a class and a confidence back. It reached 83.9% accuracy on held-out test data (loss 0.49).

What it doesn't do. Look at the training curve. Training accuracy runs to 97% while validation stalls around 76% — the model is memorising its dataset, and 83.9% on test is nowhere near the bar you'd want before calling someone's video fake. Compression and unfamiliar generation methods break it. More data and real regularisation would beat any further architecture tinkering.

Cryptography · A strange idea, finished

AES PixelVault — text hidden as video static

Encrypt text with AES-128 in CBC mode, take the ciphertext down to its bits, and paint those bits as black and white pixels. Concatenate the frames and you have a video file that is indistinguishable from static — but decodes back, exactly, with the key.

The image on the right is a real output frame holding an encrypted case document. Nothing about it says "document", which is the entire point.

What it is and isn't. A working experiment in steganographic storage, not a security product — the pixel encoding is obfuscation on top of the AES, not extra strength, and any lossy re-encode of the video destroys the payload.
